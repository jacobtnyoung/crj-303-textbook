Part II: Statistical Inference

Part I focused on describing data. We learned how variables are measured, how distributions can be organized and visualized, and how measures of central tendency and variation summarize what has been observed.

Those tools answer questions such as:

  • What values appear in the data?
  • What is a typical value?
  • How much do observations differ?
  • What is the shape of the distribution?

Part II makes an important transition. We will use data from a sample to reason about a larger population. This process is called statistical inference.

From Description to Inference

Descriptive statistics summarize the cases in front of us. Inferential statistics ask what those cases may tell us about cases we have not observed.

Suppose a survey finds that 62% of sampled residents trust the police. The percentage describes the sample. We may also want to know whether it provides a reasonable estimate of trust among all residents in the city.

That move from the sample to the population creates uncertainty. Another random sample would probably produce a somewhat different percentage. Statistical inference does not eliminate this uncertainty. Instead, it gives us tools for describing, measuring, and incorporating it into our conclusions.

ImportantThe central problem of Part II

Samples vary. Inferential statistics help us distinguish patterns that could reasonably result from sampling variation from patterns that would be unusual under a particular assumption.

The sequence can be summarized as:

  1. Describe uncertainty using probability.
  2. Represent probability with a distribution.
  3. Examine how statistics vary across samples.
  4. Use that expected variation to estimate population values.
  5. Use it to test population claims.
  6. Use the t-distribution when population variability is unknown.
  7. Extend the logic to comparisons between two independent means.

Populations, Samples, Parameters, and Statistics

Several distinctions will appear throughout Part II:

  • A population is the complete group we want to understand.
  • A sample is the subset of cases actually observed.
  • A parameter is a numerical description of a population.
  • A statistic is a numerical description calculated from a sample.

For example, the true mean response time for all calls in a city is a population parameter. The mean calculated from 100 sampled calls is a sample statistic.

In most research settings, the population parameter is unknown. The sample statistic is used to estimate it or evaluate a claim about it.

NoteInference depends on more than a formula

An inferential calculation is useful only when the sample, measurement process, and assumptions justify connecting the observed data to the population named in the conclusion.

A Recurring Reasoning Process

Although the calculations will change, the chapters repeatedly use the same underlying questions:

  1. What is the population?
    Identify the cases to which the conclusion is intended to apply.

  2. What is the sample?
    Identify the cases that were actually observed and how they were selected.

  3. What parameter is being estimated or tested?
    Determine whether the question concerns a population mean, proportion, difference, or another quantity.

  4. What statistic was observed?
    Calculate the corresponding value using the sample.

  5. How much sampling variability should we expect?
    Use the standard error to describe how statistics would vary across random samples.

  6. Is the result common or unusual under the model?
    Use a probability distribution, confidence interval, critical value, or p-value.

  7. What conclusion is supported?
    Interpret the result in the language of the research question.

  8. What cannot be concluded?
    Consider sampling, measurement, assumptions, practical importance, and whether the design supports a causal claim.

Estimation and Testing Are Connected

Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests may initially appear to be different techniques, but they rely on the same foundation.

A confidence interval begins with a sample statistic and identifies a range of plausible values for a population parameter.

A hypothesis test begins with a proposed population value and asks whether the observed statistic would be unusual if that proposal were correct.

Both require us to understand:

  • sampling variability;
  • standard errors;
  • probability distributions; and
  • the difference between statistical evidence and certainty.

Statistical Conclusions Require Care

Part II introduces formal calculations, but a correct calculation can still support a poor conclusion. Throughout these chapters, keep the following distinctions in mind:

  • Statistical significance is not practical importance. A small difference can be statistically significant when a sample is large.
  • Failure to reject is not proof. Insufficient evidence against a claim does not establish that the claim is true.
  • A narrow interval is not protection against bias. Large samples reduce random variation but do not repair poor sampling or measurement.
  • Association is not causation. A difference between groups does not establish why the groups differ.
  • Probability is not certainty. Unusual outcomes remain possible, and common outcomes are not guaranteed.
WarningInference does not rescue weak data

The methods in Part II quantify uncertainty created by random sampling. They do not automatically account for selection bias, missing data, inaccurate measurement, violated assumptions, or weak research design.

How to Work Through Part II

The material is cumulative. Ideas introduced early in the part reappear in later chapters. When solving a problem:

  1. Write down the population, sample, parameter, and statistic.
  2. Draw the relevant distribution when an area or tail is involved.
  3. Keep formulas in symbolic form until the needed values are identified.
  4. Label each result, especially the standard error and test statistic.
  5. Finish every calculation with a plain-language interpretation.
  6. State at least one limitation or unsupported conclusion.

The appendices provide:

  • the Standard Normal Distribution Table and
  • the t-Distribution Critical Values; and
  • Chi-Square Distribution Critical Values.

The normal and t tables will be used directly in Part II. The chi-square table is included as a reference for later applications and extensions.

What You Should Be Able to Do by the End

By the end of Part II, you should be able to:

  • explain why samples from the same population produce different results;
  • use probability distributions to identify common and unusual outcomes;
  • interpret z-scores, standard errors, confidence intervals, and p-values;
  • distinguish parameters from statistics;
  • construct and interpret confidence intervals;
  • test a claim about a population mean using the t-distribution;
  • compare the means of two independent groups;
  • distinguish statistical significance from substantive importance; and
  • identify the assumptions and limitations accompanying an inferential conclusion.

The goal is not simply to calculate a statistic. It is to develop a disciplined way of reasoning from incomplete information while being clear about the uncertainty that remains.
