2  Measuring Variables

2.1 The Question

NoteCentral question

How do we turn an idea into a variable that can be recorded and analyzed?

Criminal justice data describe people, incidents, places, organizations, and decisions. A dataset might record the type of crime reported, the neighborhood where it occurred, the amount of property damage, or the time an officer took to respond. Although all of these appear as columns in a dataset, they do not contain the same kind of information.

Some variables place observations into categories. Others arrange observations in a meaningful order. Still others record numerical amounts for which differences—or even ratios—have meaningful interpretations. Recognizing these differences is an important first step in selecting an appropriate statistical analysis.

2.2 What You Will Learn

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. Explain what it means to measure a characteristic with a variable.
  2. Distinguish among nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio levels of measurement.
  3. Justify the level of measurement assigned to a variable.
  4. Explain why the level of measurement affects the statistical summaries and comparisons we can make.

2.3 What Does It Mean to Measure Something?

When we measure something, we use a rule to assign a value to an observation. Consider an incident in a police-records system. The system might assign that incident:

  • an incident type such as burglary;
  • a severity rating such as high;
  • a response time of 12 minutes; and
  • an estimated property loss of $800.

Each assigned value communicates information, but the information has a different structure. Burglary is a category. High indicates an ordered position. 12 minutes and $800 represent measured quantities.

The level of measurement describes the kinds of comparisons that the values of a variable permit. The four commonly recognized levels are nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio.

TipAsk these questions

When determining a variable’s level of measurement, ask:

  1. Are the values simply different categories?
  2. Can the values be placed in a meaningful order?
  3. Are the distances between values equal?
  4. Does zero represent the absence of the quantity?

2.4 The Four Levels of Measurement

2.4.1 Nominal: Categories Without an Order

A nominal variable places observations into categories. The categories are different from one another, but they do not have an inherent order.

Examples include:

  • type of crime: assault, theft, or fraud;
  • neighborhood: Downtown, North End, or Eastside;
  • season: fall, winter, spring, or summer; and
  • arrest made: yes or no.

We cannot say that fraud is more than assault or that Downtown is higher than the North End. The labels identify different groups; they do not rank them.

Numbers do not necessarily make a variable quantitative. A police department might code burglary as 1, robbery as 2, and fraud as 3. Those numbers are labels. Fraud is not three times as much as burglary, and calculating their average would not be meaningful.

ImportantThe coding does not determine the measurement level

A variable’s level of measurement depends on what its values represent, not on whether the values happen to be written as words or numbers.

2.4.2 Ordinal: Categories With an Order

An ordinal variable places observations into categories that have a meaningful order. However, the distance between consecutive categories is not necessarily equal.

Examples include:

  • trust in the police: poor, fair, good, or excellent;
  • crime severity: low, moderate, or high;
  • fear of crime: low, medium, or high; and
  • inmate security level: minimum, medium, or maximum.

We know that high crime severity represents more severity than low crime severity. We do not know that the difference between low and moderate is identical to the difference between moderate and high.

A rating scale written with numbers can still be ordinal. For example, suppose trust in the police is recorded from 1 = Poor to 5 = Excellent. A score of 4 indicates more trust than a score of 2, but it does not necessarily indicate exactly twice as much trust.

2.4.3 Interval: Equal Distances Without a True Zero

An interval variable has ordered numerical values with equal distances between them. A one-unit change represents the same amount anywhere on the scale. However, zero does not represent the complete absence of the quantity.

Temperature measured in degrees Fahrenheit is a familiar example. The difference between 50°F and 60°F is the same as the difference between 70°F and 80°F: ten degrees. However, 0°F does not represent the absence of temperature. Because the zero point is arbitrary, ratios are not meaningful. For example, 80°F is not twice the absolute temperature of 40°F, even though 80 is twice 40.

Calendar year is another interval-level example. The difference between 2000 and 2010 is ten years, just as the difference between 2010 and 2020 is ten years. The year zero is a location on a calendar rather than the absence of time.

2.4.4 Ratio: Equal Distances and a True Zero

A ratio variable has all the properties of an interval variable and also has a meaningful zero point. Zero represents the absence of the quantity being measured, so ratio comparisons are meaningful.

Examples include:

  • number of arrests;
  • incident response time in minutes;
  • property damage in dollars;
  • victim age;
  • sentence length in years; and
  • number of prior convictions.

If one response took 20 minutes and another took 10 minutes, the first took twice as long. If one neighborhood recorded 50 crimes and another recorded 25, the first recorded twice as many crimes. These comparisons are possible because time and counts have meaningful zero points.

2.5 Comparing the Four Levels

Level Categories differ Values have an order Equal intervals Meaningful zero Example
Nominal Yes No No No Incident type
Ordinal Yes Yes No No Crime severity
Interval Yes Yes Yes No Temperature in °F
Ratio Yes Yes Yes Yes Response time

The levels build on one another. Ordinal variables contain the distinction available in nominal variables and add order. Interval variables add equal spacing. Ratio variables add a meaningful zero.

2.6 Why the Level of Measurement Matters

The level of measurement affects what we can reasonably do with a variable.

  • It helps determine which summaries and statistical procedures are appropriate.
  • It shapes how we describe differences between observations.
  • It tells us whether an average, distance, or ratio has a meaningful interpretation.
  • It prevents us from treating numerical labels as measured quantities.

For example, the most common incident type may be useful, but the average incident type is not. The median security level may communicate a meaningful ordered position, but saying that maximum security is three times minimum security would not. A mean response time, by contrast, has a clear interpretation.

WarningA common mistake

Do not classify a variable solely by looking at its stored values. Always ask what those values represent.

2.10 Chapter Summary

  • Measurement assigns values to observations according to a rule.
  • Nominal variables distinguish among categories without ordering them.
  • Ordinal variables place categories in a meaningful order, but the spacing between categories is not necessarily equal.
  • Interval variables have meaningful order and equal spacing, but they do not have a true zero.
  • Ratio variables have meaningful order, equal spacing, and a true zero.
  • A variable’s level of measurement depends on what its values represent, not on how those values are written or coded.
  • Recognizing the level of measurement helps us select appropriate summaries, comparisons, visualizations, and statistical procedures.

2.11 Key Terms

  • Interval variable: A variable with ordered values and equal intervals but no meaningful zero.
  • Level of measurement: The mathematical structure of a variable and the comparisons its values permit.
  • Measurement: The use of a rule to assign a value to an observation.
  • Nominal variable: A variable whose values identify categories without an inherent order.
  • Ordinal variable: A variable whose values identify categories with a meaningful order but without demonstrably equal spacing.
  • Ratio variable: A variable with ordered values, equal intervals, and a meaningful zero.
  • Variable: A characteristic that can take different values across observations.
