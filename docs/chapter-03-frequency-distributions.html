3  Organizing Data with Frequency Distributions

3.1 The Question

NoteCentral question

How can we turn a list of raw observations into a clear description of a variable?

Criminal justice researchers and analysts often begin with a set of individual observations. A survey may contain one response from every participant, an incident file may contain one row for every reported crime, and a correctional dataset may contain one record for every person admitted to a facility.

Raw data preserve the individual observations, but they can make the overall pattern difficult to see. A frequency distribution organizes those observations so we can determine which values are common, how much of the dataset falls into each category, and (when the categories have a meaningful order) how observations accumulate across the distribution.

3.2 What You Will Learn

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. Distinguish raw data from a frequency distribution.
  2. Calculate and interpret frequencies, proportions, and percentages.
  3. Calculate and interpret cumulative frequencies and cumulative proportions for ordered variables.
  4. Explain how a variable’s level of measurement affects the frequency table we can construct.
  5. Use a frequency distribution to answer a substantive criminal justice question.

3.3 From Raw Data to an Organized Distribution

Suppose we want to answer the following question:

How much public support is there for expanding rehabilitation programs for people in prison?

We ask 10 people: How much do you support expanding rehabilitation programs for people in prison? Responses are recorded using this scale:

  1. Strongly oppose
  2. Oppose
  3. Support
  4. Strongly support

The individual responses are the raw data:

Respondent Support for rehabilitation
1 2
2 3
3 2
4 4
5 3
6 3
7 1
8 4
9 2
10 3

Each row represents one respondent, and each recorded value represents that person’s response category. We could inspect the rows one at a time, but organizing the responses into a frequency distribution makes the overall pattern easier to understand.

ImportantThe numbers represent ordered categories

The response values 1 through 4 are codes for an ordinal variable. A higher value represents more support, but we cannot assume that the distance between every pair of response categories is equal.

3.4 Frequency

The frequency, written as \(f\), is the number of observations with a particular value. We find the frequency by counting how many respondents selected each category.

Response Response category \(f\)
1 Strongly oppose 1
2 Oppose 3
3 Support 4
4 Strongly support 2
Total 10

This table makes two facts immediately visible: Support is the most common response, and only one respondent selected Strongly oppose.

TipCheck the total

The frequencies should add to the total number of observations. Here, \(1 + 3 + 4 + 2 = 10\).

3.5 Proportion and Percentage

A proportion, written as \(p\), is the share of all observations that falls into a particular category. It is calculated by dividing a category’s frequency by the total number of observations:

\[ p = \frac{f}{N} \]

For this chapter, \(N\) represents the total number of cases in the dataset. Later, when we distinguish samples from populations, we will use \(n\) for sample size and \(N\) for population size.

Response \(f\) Calculation \(p\)
1 1 \(1/10\) 0.10
2 3 \(3/10\) 0.30
3 4 \(4/10\) 0.40
4 2 \(2/10\) 0.20
Total 10 1.00

A percentage expresses the same share out of 100:

\[ \text{Percentage} = p \times 100 \]

Thus, a proportion of 0.40 is equivalent to 40%. In this survey, 60% of respondents selected either Support or Strongly support.

TipCheck the proportion or percentage total

Proportions should add to 1.00, and percentages should add to 100%. Small differences can occur when values have been rounded.

3.6 Cumulative Frequency and Cumulative Proportion

When a variable’s categories have a meaningful order, we can calculate running totals.

The cumulative frequency, written here as \(cf\), is the number of observations at or below a particular category. The cumulative proportion, written as \(cp\), is the corresponding running share of observations.

Response \(f\) \(p\) \(cf\) \(cp\) Cumulative percentage
1 1 0.10 1 0.10 10%
2 3 0.30 4 0.40 40%
3 4 0.40 8 0.80 80%
4 2 0.20 10 1.00 100%

The second row tells us that four respondents—or 40% of the group—selected either Strongly oppose or Oppose. The third row tells us that eight respondents—or 80%—selected Support or a lower response.

WarningCumulative summaries require an order

Cumulative frequency and cumulative proportion are meaningful only when the values can be arranged from lower to higher. They are appropriate for ordinal, interval, and ratio variables. They are generally not meaningful for nominal variables such as neighborhood or incident type, because changing the arbitrary category order would change the cumulative totals.

3.7 One Table, Several Kinds of Information

Each column answers a different question:

Summary Question answered
Frequency How many observations have this value?
Proportion What share of the observations has this value?
Percentage What share out of 100 has this value?
Cumulative frequency How many observations are at or below this ordered value?
Cumulative proportion What share of observations is at or below this ordered value?

Frequencies communicate counts. Proportions and percentages make it easier to compare distributions with different totals. Cumulative summaries show where observations fall within an ordered distribution.

3.8 Check Your Understanding

Suppose 25 respondents were asked how safe they feel in their neighborhood. Ten selected Very safe. Five selected Somewhat safe.

  1. What is the frequency for Very safe?
  2. What proportion selected Very safe?
  3. What percentage selected Somewhat safe?
  4. What additional information would you need to calculate the cumulative percentage through Somewhat safe?
NoteCheck your answers
  1. The frequency for Very safe is 10.
  2. The proportion is \(10/25 = 0.40\).
  3. The percentage is \((5/25) \times 100 = 20\%\).
  4. You would need the frequencies for every response category ordered below Somewhat safe. A cumulative percentage includes the selected category and all categories below it.

3.11 Chapter Summary

  • Raw data display individual observations, while a frequency distribution organizes values into a summary.
  • A frequency is the number of observations with a particular value.
  • A proportion is a frequency divided by the total number of observations; a percentage expresses that proportion out of 100.
  • Cumulative frequency is a running count, while cumulative proportion is a running share.
  • Cumulative summaries are meaningful only when a variable’s values have a meaningful order.
  • A useful frequency table should do more than present numbers: it should help answer a substantive question.

3.12 Key Terms

  • Cumulative frequency: The number of observations at or below a particular ordered value.
  • Cumulative percentage: The percentage of observations at or below a particular ordered value.
  • Cumulative proportion: The proportion of observations at or below a particular ordered value.
  • Frequency: The number of observations with a particular value.
  • Frequency distribution: A table showing how observations are distributed across the values of a variable.
  • Percentage: A proportion expressed out of 100.
  • Proportion: The fraction of all observations with a particular value.
  • Raw data: Data presented as individual, ungrouped observations.
