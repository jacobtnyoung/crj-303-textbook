13  Comparing Two Means

13.1 The Question

NoteCentral question

Is the difference between two sample means larger than we would expect from sampling variability?

So far, we have been examining questions that involve one sample mean or one sample proportion with a proposed population value. Many questions in criminology and criminal justice go beyond this and compare two groups. We may want to know whether two programs have different mean outcomes, whether case processing differs across types of legal representation, or whether officers in different assignments have different mean caseloads.

An independent-samples t-test extends the same logic to the difference between two means. It compares the observed difference with the amount of variation expected from two independent samples.

13.2 What You Will Learn

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. State null and alternative hypotheses comparing two population means.
  2. Distinguish independent samples from paired or repeated observations.
  3. Calculate the standard error of a difference between two sample means.
  4. Calculate and interpret an obtained t-statistic.
  5. Select an approximate critical value from the t table.
  6. Make and interpret a hypothesis-test decision.
  7. Explain why a statistically significant difference does not establish causation.

Appendix B, Student’s t-Distribution Critical Values, provides the critical values used in this chapter.

13.3 Comparing Two Population Means

Many criminal justice questions compare two groups:

  • Do public defender and private attorney cases have different disposition times?
  • Do specialist and general-assignment probation officers have different caseloads?
  • Do two programs produce different mean outcomes?

For two independent groups, the null hypothesis usually states that the population means are equal:

\[ H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2 \]

This is equivalent to:

\[ H_0:\mu_1-\mu_2=0 \]

Possible alternatives are:

\[ H_A:\mu_1\ne\mu_2 \]

\[ H_A:\mu_1>\mu_2 \]

or:

\[ H_A:\mu_1<\mu_2 \]

13.4 Standard Error for the Difference

Each sample contributes uncertainty. The estimated standard error of the difference is:

\[ \widehat{SE}_{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2} =\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}} \]

The test statistic is:

\[ t=\frac{(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2)-0} {\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}} \]

The zero represents the difference proposed by \(H_0\).

WarningSquare the standard deviations

The standard-error formula uses the sample variances, \(s_1^2\) and \(s_2^2\). A common mistake is to insert the standard deviations without squaring them.

13.5 Degrees of Freedom for Two Samples

For hand calculations in this chapter, we use the approximation:

\[ df=n_1+n_2-2 \]

13.6 Worked Example: Time to Disposition

The court compares days from filing to disposition for two independent samples:

Group \(n\) Mean Standard deviation
Public defender cases 9 42 3
Private attorney cases 9 36 3

The question asks whether the population means differ:

\[ H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2 \]

\[ H_A:\mu_1\ne\mu_2 \]

13.6.1 Step 1: Calculate the Standard Error

\[ \widehat{SE}_{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2} =\sqrt{\frac{3^2}{9}+\frac{3^2}{9}} =\sqrt{2} =1.414 \]

13.6.2 Step 2: Calculate the Obtained Statistic

\[ t=\frac{42-36}{1.414}=4.24 \]

13.6.3 Step 3: Find the Critical Value

Using the approximation:

\[ df=9+9-2=16 \]

For a two-tailed test with \(\alpha=0.05\):

\[ t_{critical}=\pm2.120 \]

13.6.4 Step 4: Decide and Interpret

Because:

\[ |4.24|>2.120 \]

we reject \(H_0\). The samples provide evidence that mean disposition time differs between public defender and private attorney cases.

The positive sign indicates that the first sample mean is higher. It does not explain why the groups differ or establish that attorney type caused the difference.

13.7 Assumptions and Study Design

The calculations alone do not guarantee a valid conclusion. Ask whether:

  • the observations within and between groups are sufficiently independent;
  • the samples were selected through a defensible process;
  • the outcome is quantitative and measured consistently;
  • severe skew or influential outliers make a mean comparison misleading; and
  • the two groups represent the populations named in the conclusion.

An independent-samples test is not appropriate for repeated observations from the same people or naturally matched pairs. Those designs require a paired procedure.

ImportantA significant difference is not necessarily causal

A two-sample test evaluates evidence of a population difference. Causal interpretation requires a research design that rules out credible alternative explanations.

13.8 Check Your Understanding

Suppose two independent samples have the following results:

Group \(n\) Mean Standard deviation
Group 1 10 24 4
Group 2 10 20 4
  1. State two-tailed null and alternative hypotheses.
  2. Calculate the standard error of the difference.
  3. Calculate the obtained t.
  4. Using the classroom degrees-of-freedom approximation and \(\alpha=0.05\), make a decision.
NoteCheck your answers

\[ H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2 \]

\[ H_A:\mu_1\ne\mu_2 \]

\[ SE=\sqrt{\frac{4^2}{10}+\frac{4^2}{10}} =\sqrt{3.2} =1.789 \]

\[ t=\frac{24-20}{1.789}=2.24 \]

Using \(df=10+10-2=18\), the two-tailed critical value is \(\pm2.101\). Because \(|2.24|>2.101\), we reject \(H_0\).

13.9 Test Your Knowledge: Probation Officer Caseloads

13.9.1 Part 1: Any Difference?

A supervisor asks whether specialist and general-assignment probation officers have different population mean caseloads.

Group \(n\) Mean Standard deviation
Specialist 8 15 2
General assignment 16 13 4

Using \(\alpha=0.05\):

  1. State \(H_0\) and \(H_A\).
  2. Identify the type of test.
  3. Calculate the standard error and obtained t.
  4. Calculate the approximate degrees of freedom and find the critical value.
  5. Reject or fail to reject \(H_0\).
  6. Interpret the result.

13.9.2 Part 2: Higher Specialist Caseloads?

The supervisor instead asks whether specialists have a higher population mean caseload.

Group \(n\) Mean Standard deviation
Specialist 16 18 4
General assignment 10 14 5

Repeat the six steps using \(\alpha=0.05\).

NoteReview the answers

13.9.3 Part 1

\[ H_0:\mu_{specialist}=\mu_{general} \]

\[ H_A:\mu_{specialist}\ne\mu_{general} \]

This is a two-tailed test.

\[ SE=\sqrt{\frac{2^2}{8}+\frac{4^2}{16}} =\sqrt{1.5} =1.225 \]

\[ t=\frac{15-13}{1.225}=1.63 \]

Using the classroom approximation, \(df=8+16-2=22\) and \(t_{critical}=\pm2.074\). We fail to reject \(H_0\). The sample does not provide sufficient evidence that the population mean caseloads differ.

13.9.4 Part 2

\[ H_0:\mu_{specialist}=\mu_{general} \]

\[ H_A:\mu_{specialist}>\mu_{general} \]

This is a right-tailed test.

\[ SE=\sqrt{\frac{4^2}{16}+\frac{5^2}{10}} =\sqrt{3.5} =1.871 \]

\[ t=\frac{18-14}{1.871}=2.14 \]

Using the approximation, \(df=16+10-2=24\) and \(t_{critical}=1.711\). We reject \(H_0\). The sample provides evidence that specialists have a higher population mean caseload.

Neither result establishes that officer assignment causes caseload differences.

13.12 Part II: What You Can Now Do

Part II began with probability and developed a sequence of ideas:

  1. Probability describes uncertainty.
  2. The normal distribution helps locate common and unusual outcomes.
  3. Sampling distributions describe how statistics vary across samples.
  4. Confidence intervals estimate unknown population parameters.
  5. Hypothesis tests evaluate population claims.
  6. The t-distribution supports inference about a population mean when variability is unknown.
  7. Independent-samples t-tests evaluate differences between two population means.

These tools do not remove uncertainty. They make uncertainty visible and allow it to be incorporated into statistical reasoning.

13.13 Chapter Summary

  • An independent-samples t-test evaluates a difference between two population means.
  • The null hypothesis usually states that the difference between population means is zero.
  • The standard error incorporates the sample sizes and variabilities of both groups.
  • The obtained t-statistic expresses the observed difference in estimated standard-error units.
  • This chapter uses \(n_1+n_2-2\) as a classroom degrees-of-freedom approximation.
  • Statistical software commonly uses Welch’s adjusted degrees of freedom.
  • Independent-samples tests are not appropriate for repeated or naturally paired observations.
  • Statistical significance does not establish practical importance or causation.

13.14 Key Terms

  • Independent samples: Samples containing different, unrelated cases.
  • Independent-samples t-test: A test comparing the means of two independent populations.
  • Mean difference: The difference between two sample or population means.
  • Paired observations: Measurements that are linked through repeated observations or natural matching.
  • Standard error of the difference: The expected sampling variability of the difference between two sample means.
