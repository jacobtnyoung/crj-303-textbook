14  Association and Bivariate Tables

14.1 The Question

NoteCentral question

How can we use a table to describe the relationship between two categorical variables?

Earlier chapters examined one variable at a time. We calculated the proportion of defendants with prior records, identified the most common case outcome, and compared mean outcomes between groups. Those analyses are useful, but many criminal justice questions concern how two variables are related.

For example, a court analyst might ask whether defendants with prior records are more likely to be convicted. Answering that question requires more than a frequency distribution for prior record and a separate distribution for case outcome. The analyst must examine the two variables together.

This chapter introduces bivariate tables, which organize two categorical variables in a single table. These tables allow us to compare groups, identify patterns, and describe associations.

14.2 What You Will Learn

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. Distinguish univariate questions from bivariate questions.
  2. Construct a bivariate table from case-level data.
  3. Identify cells, row totals, column totals, and the grand total.
  4. Calculate row and column proportions.
  5. Select the proportions that answer a particular research question.
  6. Describe an association between two categorical variables.
  7. Explain why an observed association does not by itself establish causation.

14.3 From One Variable to Two

A univariate analysis examines one variable. Suppose a court analyst reviews 20 cases and reports that:

  • 40% of the defendants had a prior criminal record; and
  • 65% of the cases resulted in conviction.

These statements describe the separate distributions of two variables. They do not tell us whether defendants with prior records were more likely to be convicted.

A bivariate analysis examines two variables together. It asks whether the distribution of one variable differs across the categories of another variable. Examples include:

  • Is bail release related to employment status?
  • Does enforcement outcome differ by incident type?
  • Is participation in a treatment program associated with rearrest?
  • Does public confidence in police differ across neighborhoods?

Each question involves two variables. When both variables are categorical, a bivariate table is a useful starting point.

14.4 Bivariate Association

Two variables are associated when the distribution of one variable differs across categories of the other. If conviction rates differ between defendants with and without prior records, prior-record status and case outcome are associated in the observed data.

If the conviction rates are the same for both groups, the variables show no association in the table. IN other words, knowing a defendant’s prior-record status would not help us anticipate the distribution of case outcomes.

Association is about a pattern of differences. It does not necessarily mean that one variable causes the other. It simply tells us that the variables are related.

ImportantAssociation is not causation

A bivariate table can show that two variables are associated. It cannot, by itself, establish that one variable caused the other. Other characteristics, selection processes, measurement decisions, and institutional practices may help explain the observed pattern.

14.5 Constructing a Bivariate Table

Consider a sample of 20 criminal cases. The two variables are Prior Criminal Record and Case Outcome.

To construct a bivariate table:

  1. Select one variable for the rows.
  2. Select the other variable for the columns.
  3. Count the cases with each combination of values.
  4. Add the row totals, column totals, and grand total.

Using prior record as the row variable and case outcome as the column variable produces the following table:

Prior criminal record Convicted Not convicted Total
Yes 7 1 8
No 6 6 12
Total 13 7 20

Each interior value is a cell frequency. The upper-left cell contains the seven cases in which the defendant had a prior record and was convicted.

The totals at the edges of the table are marginal totals:

  • The row totals show that 8 defendants had prior records and 12 did not.
  • The column totals show that 13 defendants were convicted and 7 were not.
  • The grand total is the complete sample of 20 cases.

The marginal totals reproduce the separate frequency distributions (remember frequency distributions!). The interior cells show how the two variables occur together.

TipCheck the table before interpreting it

The interior cells must add to the grand total. Each row total must equal the sum of the cells in that row, and each column total must equal the sum of the cells in that column.

14.6 Why Frequencies Are Often Not Enough

The counts in the table suggest that defendants with prior records were frequently convicted: seven defendants with prior records were convicted, compared with six defendants without prior records. However, the groups are different sizes. There are only 8 defendants with prior records but 12 without them.

Comparing the counts 7 and 6 ignores that difference. Proportions place the groups on a common scale and provide a clearer comparison.

The correct denominator depends on the question. We can calculate either row proportions or column proportions, but they answer different questions.

14.7 Row Proportions

A row proportion divides each cell by its row total:

\[ \text{Row proportion}=\frac{\text{cell frequency}}{\text{row total}} \]

Row proportions describe the distribution of the column variable within each category of the row variable. Each row sums to 1.00, or 100% if we are using percentages.

For defendants with prior records:

\[ P(\text{Convicted}\mid\text{Prior record})=\frac{7}{8}=0.875 \]

The vertical bar can be read as “given.” The result is the proportion convicted given that the defendant had a prior record.

The complete row-proportion table is:

Prior criminal record Convicted Not convicted Total
Yes 0.875 0.125 1.00
No 0.500 0.500 1.00

This table answers the question: Given a defendant’s prior-record status, what proportion were convicted or not convicted?

  • Among defendants with prior records, 87.5% were convicted.
  • Among defendants without prior records, 50.0% were convicted.

The conviction proportion is 37.5 percentage points higher among defendants with prior records:

\[ 87.5\%-50.0\%=37.5\text{ percentage points} \]

The differing proportions indicate an association in this sample.

WarningCompare percentages, not percentage change

Subtracting 50.0% from 87.5% gives a difference of 37.5 percentage points. Percentage points describe the absolute difference between two percentages. They are not the same as a percent increase.

14.8 Column Proportions

A column proportion divides each cell by its column total:

\[ \text{Column proportion}=\frac{\text{cell frequency}}{\text{column total}} \]

Column proportions describe the distribution of the row variable within each category of the column variable. Each column sums to 1.00.

Prior criminal record Convicted Not convicted
Yes 0.538 0.143
No 0.462 0.857
Total 1.00 1.00

This table answers a different question: Given the case outcome, what proportion of defendants had a prior record?

  • Among convicted defendants, 53.8% had a prior record.
  • Among defendants who were not convicted, 14.3% had a prior record.

These statements are correct, but they do not directly answer whether defendants with prior records were more likely to be convicted. That question requires conviction proportions within the prior-record groups, so the row proportions are the more direct choice.

14.9 Choosing the Correct Proportions

The placement of variables should follow the question rather than a mechanical rule. A useful arrangement is:

  • place the grouping or explanatory variable in the rows;
  • place the response or outcome variable in the columns; and
  • compare the outcome proportions across rows.

With this arrangement, row proportions usually answer the substantive question.

Research question Groups being compared Outcome Useful calculation
Does bail release differ by employment status? Employment groups Bail decision Row proportions with employment in rows
Does enforcement outcome differ by incident type? Incident types Warning or citation Row proportions with incident type in rows
Among cited students, what proportion were undergraduates? Outcome groups Student status Column proportions if outcomes are columns
TipRead the denominator in words

Ask, “Within which groups do I want the percentages to sum to 100%?” Those groups provide the denominator. If you want to compare outcomes within employment groups, divide by the employment-group totals.

Reversing the rows and columns does not change the underlying data or association. It changes which proportions are easiest to calculate and interpret.

14.10 Describing an Association

A clear interpretation should identify:

  1. the groups being compared;
  2. the outcome being examined;
  3. the relevant proportions;
  4. the direction and size of the difference; and
  5. the population to which the evidence applies.

For the court example, an appropriate description is:

In this sample, 87.5% of defendants with prior records were convicted, compared with 50.0% of defendants without prior records. Conviction was therefore 37.5 percentage points more common among defendants with prior records.

This statement describes the pattern without making an unsupported causal claim or assuming that the sample necessarily represents every case in the court system.

Avoid interpretations such as:

  • “A prior record caused the conviction.”
  • “Judges discriminated against defendants with records.”
  • “Prior record is the only reason the groups differ.”

Those explanations may be important research questions, but the bivariate table alone cannot answer them.

14.11 Check Your Understanding

A campus police department records the following encounters:

Incident type Warning Citation Total
Alcohol-related 25 37 62
Non-alcohol-related 51 7 58
  1. Which variable is the row variable?
  2. What proportion of alcohol-related incidents resulted in a citation?
  3. What proportion of non-alcohol-related incidents resulted in a citation?
  4. Does the table suggest an association between incident type and enforcement outcome?
NoteCheck your answers
  1. Incident type is the row variable. Enforcement outcome is the column variable.
  2. For alcohol-related incidents, the citation proportion is \(37/62=0.597\), or 59.7%.
  3. For non-alcohol-related incidents, the citation proportion is \(7/58=0.121\), or 12.1%.
  4. Yes. Citation was 47.6 percentage points more common among alcohol-related incidents. This is a substantial descriptive difference in the observed encounters. The table does not establish that incident type caused the enforcement decision.

14.12.2 Your Task

Use employment status as the row variable and bail decision as the column variable.

  1. Construct a bivariate table of frequencies, including marginal totals.
  2. Convert the interior cells to row proportions.
  3. Among employed defendants, what proportion were released?
  4. Among unemployed defendants, what proportion were released?
  5. Describe the association between employment status and bail decision.
  6. Identify one conclusion the table does not support.
NoteReview the answers

The frequency table is:

Employment status Released Detained Total
Employed 6 1 7
Unemployed 2 5 7
Total 8 6 14

The row-proportion table is:

Employment status Released Detained Total
Employed 0.857 0.143 1.00
Unemployed 0.286 0.714 1.00

Among employed defendants, \(6/7=0.857\), or 85.7%, were released. Among unemployed defendants, \(2/7=0.286\), or 28.6%, were released. Release was 57.1 percentage points more common among employed defendants in this sample, indicating an association between employment status and bail decision.

The table does not establish that employment status caused the bail decision. It also does not show whether the pattern represents the larger population of defendants. Other factors—such as offense severity, prior record, or assessed risk—could be related to both employment status and the bail decision.

14.13 Data Investigation: Two Categorical Variables

Identify two categorical variables in a dataset available to you. O ne should serve as a grouping variable and the other as an outcome.

  1. State a bivariate research question.
  2. Identify the cases represented by the data.
  3. Construct a frequency table with marginal totals.
  4. Calculate the conditional proportions that answer your question.
  5. Describe the observed association in one or two sentences.
  6. State one limitation or alternative explanation that the table cannot resolve.

Before interpreting the table, consider whether missing values, small groups, or inconsistent classification could produce a misleading pattern.

14.14 Project Connection

Select two categorical variables relevant to your investigation. Write a question about whether the outcome differs across categories of the grouping variable. Construct a bivariate table and explain why row or column proportions provide the appropriate comparison.

Do not make a causal claim unless the research design supports one. At this stage, the goal is to describe the relationship visible in the data.

14.15 Looking Ahead to Chapter 15

A bivariate table can reveal that groups have different outcome proportions. It does not tell us whether a difference observed in a sample is larger than we might expect from sampling variability.

Chapter 15 introduces the chi-square test of independence. This test compares the frequencies observed in a bivariate table with the frequencies we would expect if the two variables were independent in the population.

14.16 Chapter Summary

  • Univariate analysis describes one variable, while bivariate analysis examines two variables together.
  • A bivariate table displays the frequency associated with each combination of two categorical variables.
  • Marginal totals describe the separate distributions; interior cells show how the variables occur together.
  • Row and column proportions answer different conditional questions.
  • The research question determines which proportions should be compared.
  • An association exists when the distribution of one variable differs across categories of another.
  • An observed association does not, by itself, demonstrate causation or rule out alternative explanations.

14.17 Key Terms

  • Association: A pattern in which the distribution of one variable differs across categories or values of another variable.
  • Bivariate analysis: Analysis involving two variables.
  • Bivariate table: A table displaying the joint frequencies of two categorical variables.
  • Cell frequency: The number of cases with a particular combination of values on two variables.
  • Column proportion: A cell frequency divided by its column total.
  • Conditional proportion: A proportion calculated within a specified group or category.
  • Grand total: The total number of cases represented in a table.
  • Marginal total: A row or column total shown at the edge of a bivariate table.
  • Percentage-point difference: The result of subtracting one percentage from another.
  • Row proportion: A cell frequency divided by its row total.
  • Univariate analysis: Analysis involving one variable.
