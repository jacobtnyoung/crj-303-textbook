15  The Chi-Square Test

15.1 The Question

NoteCentral question

Could the association observed in a sample have arisen through chance variation if the variables were independent?

Chapter 14 used bivariate tables and conditional proportions to describe relationships between categorical variables. If two groups have different outcome proportions, we can say that the variables are associated in the observed data.

When the cases are a sample from a larger population, however, we face another question: Does the sample provide convincing evidence that the variables are also associated in the population, or could the observed differences have arisen through random chance under independence?

The chi-square test of independence applies the logic of hypothesis testing to a bivariate table. It compares the frequencies we observe with the frequencies we would expect if the two variables were independent.

15.2 What You Will Learn

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:

  1. Distinguish a descriptive association from an inferential conclusion.
  2. State null and alternative hypotheses for a chi-square test of independence.
  3. Calculate expected frequencies for a bivariate table.
  4. Calculate the chi-square test statistic.
  5. Determine the degrees of freedom and locate a critical value.
  6. Make and interpret a hypothesis-test decision.
  7. Identify the assumptions and limitations of a chi-square test.

Appendix C, Chi-Square Distribution Critical Values, provides the critical values used in this chapter.

15.3 From Description to Inference

Suppose a police department examines whether use-of-force involvement differs by officer experience. A bivariate table and row proportions can show whether the observed rates differ:

Officer experience Use of force: Yes Use of force: No Total
0–5 years 18 7 25
6 or more years 12 38 50
Total 30 45 75

Among officers with 0–5 years of experience, \(18/25=0.72\), or 72%, were involved in a use-of-force incident. Among officers with 6 or more years of experience, \(12/50=0.24\), or 24%, were involved.

The 48-percentage-point difference is an association in this sample. It does not automatically demonstrate an association in a larger population. Even when two variables are independent in a population, random samples will rarely produce exactly equal proportions.

15.3.1 What “Random Chance” Means

Imagine repeatedly flipping a fair coin. Even though heads and tails are equally likely, a small set of flips will rarely divide perfectly in half. The difference does not necessarily mean that the coin is unfair; ordinary random chance can produce an uneven result.

The same idea applies to a bivariate table. If two variables are independent, repeated random samples will not reproduce the expected frequencies exactly. Some cases will fall into cells differently simply by chance. For sample data, statisticians call this sampling variability: the chance variation that occurs because one random sample will differ from another.

The chi-square test asks whether the observed counts depart so far from the expected counts that random chance under independence is no longer a plausible explanation.

NoteChance does not mean unexplained causation

Here, chance refers to the random variation we would expect in cell counts if the null hypothesis were true. It does not mean that the people, decisions, or events in the study occurred without causes.

15.4 Independence and the Hypotheses

Two categorical variables are independent when the distribution of one variable is the same across categories of the other. If officer experience and use-of-force incidents are independent, the proportion involved in use of force should be approximately the same for both experience groups, apart from chance variation from one sample to another.

The hypotheses for a chi-square test of independence are:

\[ H_0:\text{ The two variables are independent in the population.} \]

\[ H_A:\text{ The two variables are associated in the population.} \]

For the officer example:

\[ H_0:\text{ Officer experience and use-of-force involvement are independent.} \]

\[ H_A:\text{ Officer experience and use-of-force involvement are associated.} \]

The hypotheses refer to the population, not merely to the sample. The sample table provides the evidence used to evaluate them.

ImportantThe null hypothesis is independence

For a chi-square test of independence, the null hypothesis does not claim that every cell has the same frequency. It claims that the variables are independent. Unequal group sizes and unequal marginal totals can still produce different expected cell frequencies.

15.5 Observed and Expected Frequencies

The values recorded in the sample table are the observed frequencies, represented by \(f_O\). To evaluate independence, we compare them with expected frequencies, represented by \(f_E\).

An expected frequency is the count a cell would have under the null hypothesis, given the table’s row total, column total, and grand total. For each cell:

\[ f_E=\frac{(\text{row total})(\text{column total})}{n} \]

In the officer example, the expected frequency for officers with 0–5 years of experience who were involved in use of force is:

\[ f_E=\frac{(25)(30)}{75}=10 \]

The complete expected-frequency table is:

Officer experience Use of force: Yes Use of force: No Total
0–5 years 10 15 25
6 or more years 20 30 50
Total 30 45 75

Notice that the expected table has the same marginal totals as the observed table. Only the interior cell frequencies have changed.

Under independence, 40% of all officers were involved in use of force because \(30/75=0.40\). The expected table therefore assigns 40% of each experience group to the “Yes” column:

\[ 0.40(25)=10 \]

\[ 0.40(50)=20 \]

The observed values, 18 and 12, depart substantially from those expectations.

TipCheck the expected table

Expected frequencies should reproduce the observed row totals, column totals, and grand total. If they do not, check the calculation before continuing.

15.6 The Chi-Square Test Statistic

The chi-square statistic summarizes the difference between observed and expected frequencies across every cell:

\[ \chi^2=\sum\frac{(f_O-f_E)^2}{f_E} \]

For each cell, the calculation:

  1. subtracts the expected frequency from the observed frequency;
  2. squares the difference so positive and negative deviations do not cancel;
  3. divides by the expected frequency to account for the size of the cell; and
  4. adds the result to the contributions from all other cells.

The four cell contributions for the officer example are:

Officer experience Use of force: Yes Use of force: No
0–5 years \(\frac{(18-10)^2}{10}=6.400\) \(\frac{(7-15)^2}{15}=4.267\)
6 or more years \(\frac{(12-20)^2}{20}=3.200\) \(\frac{(38-30)^2}{30}=2.133\)

Adding the contributions gives:

\[ \chi^2=6.400+4.267+3.200+2.133=16.00 \]

The statistic cannot be negative because every deviation is squared. A value near zero means the observed and expected tables are similar. A larger value indicates a greater departure from independence.

WarningUse counts in the chi-square calculation

The chi-square statistic is calculated from observed and expected frequencies, not percentages or proportions. Conditional proportions help describe the association; frequencies are used to test it.

15.7 Degrees of Freedom and the Critical Value

The chi-square sampling distribution depends on the number of rows and columns. The degrees of freedom are:

\[ df=(r-1)(c-1) \]

where \(r\) is the number of rows and \(c\) is the number of columns.

The officer table has two rows and two columns:

\[ df=(2-1)(2-1)=1 \]

For \(df=1\) and \(\alpha=0.05\), the chi-square critical value is 3.841. The rejection region is in the right tail because larger chi-square values represent greater disagreement with the null hypothesis.

The decision rule is:

  • If \(\chi^2_{obtained}>\chi^2_{critical}\), reject \(H_0\).
  • If \(\chi^2_{obtained}\leq\chi^2_{critical}\), fail to reject \(H_0\).

For the officer example:

\[ 16.00>3.841 \]

We reject the null hypothesis. The sample provides evidence that officer experience and use-of-force involvement are associated in the population represented by the sample.

15.8 A Complete Interpretation

A useful conclusion combines the descriptive pattern with the inferential decision:

Officers with 0–5 years of experience had a higher observed use-of-force involvement rate than officers with 6 or more years of experience, 72% compared with 24%. The chi-square test was statistically significant, \(\chi^2(1)=16.00\), so we reject the null hypothesis of independence.

The conclusion should not say that experience caused the difference. The test also does not explain why the association exists.

ImportantStatistical significance is not an explanation

A significant chi-square result indicates evidence of an association. It does not establish causation, identify the mechanism producing the association, or show that the relationship is substantively important.

15.9 What Chi-Square Does Not Tell Us

The chi-square test answers a limited question: Are the variables associated in the population represented by the sample?

It does not, by itself, tell us:

  • which variable causes the other;
  • whether a third variable explains the association;
  • why the observed pattern exists;
  • whether the association is large or practically important; or
  • whether the data were measured or sampled well.

The conditional proportions remain essential because they show the direction and size of the observed differences. A chi-square decision without the underlying proportions is incomplete.

The value of \(\chi^2\) is also affected by sample size. With a large sample, relatively small differences may become statistically significant. A larger chi-square statistic should not automatically be described as a stronger or more important relationship.

15.10 Assumptions and Conditions

A chi-square test is appropriate when:

  • both variables are categorical;
  • the table contains frequency counts rather than percentages;
  • each case appears in only one cell;
  • the observations are sufficiently independent;
  • the sampling process supports inference to the population named in the conclusion; and
  • expected frequencies are not too small.

15.11 Check Your Understanding

A reentry program compares rearrest outcomes for a sample of participants and nonparticipants.

Group Rearrested Not rearrested Total
Program participants 8 22 30
Nonparticipants 15 15 30
Total 23 37 60

Using \(\alpha=0.05\):

  1. Describe the rearrest pattern using row proportions.
  2. State \(H_0\) and \(H_A\).
  3. Calculate the four expected frequencies.
  4. Calculate \(\chi^2\) and \(df\).
  5. Compare the result with the critical value and make a decision.
  6. Explain why the decision does not prove whether the program reduced rearrest.
NoteCheck your answers

The rearrest rate is \(8/30=26.7\%\) for program participants and \(15/30=50.0\%\) for nonparticipants, a difference of 23.3 percentage points.

The hypotheses are:

\[ H_0:\text{ Program participation and rearrest are independent.} \]

\[ H_A:\text{ Program participation and rearrest are associated.} \]

The expected frequencies are 11.5 rearrested and 18.5 not rearrested in each row. The statistic is:

\[ \chi^2= \frac{(8-11.5)^2}{11.5}+ \frac{(22-18.5)^2}{18.5}+ \frac{(15-11.5)^2}{11.5}+ \frac{(15-18.5)^2}{18.5} =3.45 \]

There is one degree of freedom. Because \(3.45<3.841\), we fail to reject \(H_0\). The observed difference does not reach the \(\alpha=0.05\) threshold in this sample.

Neither the descriptive difference nor a significant test would, by itself, prove a program effect. Participants and nonparticipants may differ in risk, motivation, eligibility, supervision, or other characteristics.

15.12 Test Your Knowledge 1: Sex and Offense Type

15.12.1 The Scenario

A criminologist examines whether offense type differs by sex among a random sample of 40 arrested individuals.

Sex Violent offense Nonviolent offense Total
Male 18 6 24
Female 2 14 16
Total 20 20 40

15.12.2 Your Task

Using \(\alpha=0.05\):

  1. Create a table of row proportions and describe the observed association.
  2. State the null and alternative hypotheses.
  3. Calculate the expected frequency for each cell.
  4. Calculate the chi-square statistic.
  5. Report the degrees of freedom and critical value.
  6. Make and interpret the hypothesis-test decision.
  7. State one conclusion that the result does not support.
NoteReview the answers

The row proportions are:

Sex Violent offense Nonviolent offense Total
Male 0.750 0.250 1.00
Female 0.125 0.875 1.00

Violent offenses account for 75.0% of the male cases and 12.5% of the female cases in the sample.

The hypotheses are:

\[ H_0:\text{ Sex and offense type are independent.} \]

\[ H_A:\text{ Sex and offense type are associated.} \]

The expected frequencies are:

Sex Violent offense Nonviolent offense
Male 12 12
Female 8 8

The test statistic is:

\[ \chi^2= \frac{(18-12)^2}{12}+ \frac{(6-12)^2}{12}+ \frac{(2-8)^2}{8}+ \frac{(14-8)^2}{8} =15.00 \]

With \(df=1\), the critical value is 3.841. Because \(15.00>3.841\), we reject \(H_0\). The sample provides evidence that sex and offense type are associated in the population represented by the sample.

The result does not show that sex causes offending behavior. It also does not explain the roles of reporting, enforcement, arrest, charging, or other selection processes in producing the observed cases.

15.13 Test Your Knowledge 2: Campus Police Encounters

15.13.1 The Scenario

A university police department studies 120 encounters involving three variables: student status, incident type, and enforcement outcome. It constructs two bivariate tables.

Incident Type by Student Status

Student status Alcohol-related Non-alcohol-related Total
Undergraduate 52 28 80
Graduate 10 30 40
Total 62 58 120

Enforcement Outcome by Incident Type

Incident type Warning Citation Total
Alcohol-related 25 37 62
Non-alcohol-related 51 7 58
Total 76 44 120

15.13.2 Your Task

Conduct a separate chi-square test for each table using \(\alpha=0.05\). For each test:

  1. Calculate and interpret the row proportions.
  2. State \(H_0\) and \(H_A\).
  3. Calculate the expected frequencies.
  4. Calculate \(\chi^2\) and \(df\).
  5. Make and interpret the hypothesis-test decision.
NoteReview the answers

For student status and incident type, 65.0% of undergraduate encounters and 25.0% of graduate encounters were alcohol-related.

The expected frequencies are:

Student status Alcohol-related Non-alcohol-related
Undergraduate 41.333 38.667
Graduate 20.667 19.333

The hypotheses state that student status and incident type are independent (\(H_0\)) or associated (\(H_A\)). The result is:

\[ \chi^2(1)=17.09 \]

Because \(17.09>3.841\), we reject \(H_0\). The sample provides evidence of an association between student status and incident type.

For incident type and enforcement outcome, 59.7% of alcohol-related encounters and 12.1% of non-alcohol-related encounters resulted in citations.

The expected frequencies are:

Incident type Warning Citation
Alcohol-related 39.267 22.733
Non-alcohol-related 36.733 21.267

The hypotheses state that incident type and enforcement outcome are independent (\(H_0\)) or associated (\(H_A\)). The result is:

\[ \chi^2(1)=29.25 \]

Because \(29.25>3.841\), we reject \(H_0\). The sample provides evidence of an association between incident type and enforcement outcome.

Neither result establishes causation. The tables also do not show whether the associations reflect differences in behavior, reporting, enforcement, classification, or other features of the encounter process.

15.14 Data Investigation: Testing Independence

Select two categorical variables in a dataset available to you.

  1. State a research question about their association.
  2. Construct a frequency table and calculate the relevant conditional proportions.
  3. State the null and alternative hypotheses.
  4. Calculate the expected frequencies and verify that the conditions for the test are reasonable.
  5. Calculate the chi-square statistic and degrees of freedom.
  6. Make an inferential decision using \(\alpha=0.05\).
  7. Write a conclusion that includes the descriptive pattern, inferential result, and an important limitation.

If the data were not obtained through a defensible sampling process, be careful about extending the conclusion beyond the observed cases.

15.15 Project Connection

Return to the two categorical variables examined in Chapter 14. If the data and sampling process support inference, conduct a chi-square test of independence. Report the row or column proportions, \(\chi^2\), degrees of freedom, decision, and substantive interpretation.

If inference is not justified, explain why and retain the analysis as descriptive. Recognizing when a statistical test is not warranted is part of sound statistical reasoning.

15.16 Looking Ahead to Chapter 16

Bivariate tables and chi-square tests examine relationships between categorical variables. Some research questions instead involve two quantitative variables, such as sentence length and a risk score or officer workload and the number of community contacts.

Chapter 16 introduces scatterplots and the correlation coefficient. These tools describe the direction and strength of a linear relationship between two quantitative variables.

15.17 Chapter Summary

  • A bivariate table provides a descriptive view of an association between categorical variables.
  • The chi-square test of independence evaluates whether a sample provides evidence of an association in the population.
  • Under the null hypothesis, random chance can make observed counts differ from expected counts; in sample data, this chance variation is called sampling variability.
  • Expected frequencies represent the counts predicted under the null hypothesis of independence.
  • The chi-square statistic summarizes discrepancies between observed and expected frequencies across all cells.
  • Degrees of freedom depend on the number of rows and columns.
  • A statistically significant chi-square result indicates evidence of association, not causation, explanation, or practical importance.
  • Valid inference depends on independent observations, adequate expected frequencies, and a defensible sampling process.

15.18 Key Terms

  • Chi-square distribution: A family of right-skewed sampling distributions used to evaluate chi-square statistics.
  • Chi-square test of independence: A hypothesis test assessing whether two categorical variables are associated in a population.
  • Critical value: The threshold separating results that lead to rejection of the null hypothesis from those that do not.
  • Degrees of freedom: A value determined by the number of rows and columns, calculated as \((r-1)(c-1)\).
  • Expected frequency (\(f_E\)): The cell count predicted under the null hypothesis of independence.
  • Independence: A condition in which the distribution of one variable does not differ across categories of another.
  • Observed frequency (\(f_O\)): The cell count recorded in the sample data.
  • Statistical significance: A conclusion that the observed result would be sufficiently unusual under the null hypothesis to reject it at the chosen alpha level.
